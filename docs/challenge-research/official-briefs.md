## AFTERQUERY — Break the Agent

https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/328

BY AFTERQUERY

Break the Agent
Build a reinforcement learning environment that challenges today's best AI agents, demonstrate real failure modes, and show that its rewards reflect real progress. Introduction Today's cutting-edge AI systems can solve Millennium Problems, discover new drugs, and create entire production apps with a few lines of prompting. But they still struggle in a wide variety of novel situations, ranging from everyday use cases to niche, highly technical workflows. We ask: what should AI agents be able to do that they still struggle with today? At AfterQuery, we build environments for training and testing agents. The work starts with choosing a problem worth learning, defining an action and observation space, and defining a reward function that defines what success looks like. Getting those pieces right is a heavy research and engineering problem in itself. For this challenge, we want to see your take on it. Build a world with something interesting to learn. Find out where strong agents struggle, and whether your reward actually encourages the behavior you intended. The challenge Choose a setting you find interesting and build an environment an agent can interact with. It could be a game, a scientific simulation, a software task, a real-world process, or something we haven't thought of. Explain why you chose it and what an agent could learn there. Your environment should have a clear goal, actions the agent can take, and a reward you can check. Run a frontier agent in it and compare the results with a stronger reference run your team can demonstrate and reproduce. We'll provide the agent harness and model credits. Show where the agent falls short and why. The challenge should come from the decisions it needs to make, with access to the information and tools needed to succeed. We'll also try to find shortcuts that earn a high score without completing the task: reward hacking. The setting and approach are yours to choose. We care about what makes the environment useful for learning and what your experiments reveal. Optional stretch goal Train a smaller model using experience from your environment and show whether it improves. Prizes The top three teams receive €3,000, €1,500 and €500 in cash. Members of the winning teams can go straight to the final stage of AfterQuery's interview process: a work trial with our environments team, when they're ready. Finalists also receive AfterQuery gear. How we judge: We'll look at four things: how much room there is for the agent to improve, whether its mistakes come from meaningful challenges, how well the reward holds up to exploits, and how well the environment is built and the results can be reproduced. Judging rubric • The gap (30 p) – Full points: the frontier agent consistently scores below a stronger reference run your team demonstrates; both results can be reproduced across repeated runs. Zero points: no measurable gap, or the claimed gap cannot be reproduced or checked against a demonstrated reference run. • Meaningful difficulty (30 p) – Full points: you can explain the agent's main mistakes and connect them to decisions in the task; the information and tools needed to do better are available to it. Zero points: the gap comes from unfairly withheld information, trick wording, impossible timing, or broken tools. • Reward integrity (25 p) – Full points: the reward measures success at the task and holds up when judges try to exploit it; doing nothing or stopping early earns little or no credit; completing the task well remains the best way to score. Zero points: an exploit, scoring loophole, or strategy that avoids the task earns a better score than successful play. • Craft (15 p) – Full points: the environment's rules and behavior make sense for the setting; judges can run the evaluation from your README with one command and reproduce your reported results. Zero points: the environment only works in your demo, or judges cannot reproduce the evaluation. How judging runs: Each team gets a 10-minute demo. Judges then rerun the evaluation with the standard harness: your reference run, three agent runs, a do-nothing baseline, and a stop-early baseline. They also spend 15 minutes looking for reward exploits. Scores are based on these runs. Tiebreaker: If point totals are close, we'll favor a team that shows a smaller model improving after training on experience from its environment.

## AALTO UNIVERSITY — Aaltonomous Humanoid

https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/320

BY AALTO UNIVERSITY

Aaltonomous Humanoid
Our humanoid robot can do backflips but can't sort a few blocks into boxes. Give it a brain. Introduction Humanoid robot hardware has improved enormously in recent years. Robots can now do impressive things like backflips, dancing and kung fu. They are also becoming surprisingly affordable: the Unitree G1 costs around €50,000, and it won't be long before humanoids are within reach of ordinary people. But there's a catch, known in robotics as Moravec's paradox. Hard-coded backflips are relatively easy for a robot, while something as simple as picking up a small block and putting it in the right box is much harder, even though a small child can do it without thinking. Our G1, Väiski, is physically very capable, but it's still pretty dumb: it has very little autonomy or onboard intelligence. During this hackathon, your job is to give it a brain. The task Make the G1 sort objects into boxes on its own, without a human controlling it. Väiski stands behind a counter with its legs and waist held stiff, working with two 7-DoF arms and two Dex3 three-fingered hands. In front of it, 3 to 5 printed objects lie at random positions and angles: cubes, cylinders and triangular prisms, about 4.5 cm in size, in red, yellow and blue. Behind the objects are three identical orange boxes (21 cm wide) at fixed positions: left, centre and right, in a row, 5 cm apart from each other and 40 cm from the robot's torso. The boxes have no markings; they are told apart only by their position. The robot has to put every object into the right box within 2 minutes. Fixed sorting: red objects go into the left box, yellow into the centre box, blue into the right box. Sort by instruction: a text instruction defines which box each object goes into, e.g. "Sort the objects: red into right, blue into left, yellow into center." Expect phrasings you haven't seen before. Stretch goal: put each object through the matching hole in a lid on one box. Your policy sees the head camera (colour + depth), both wrist cameras, the joint states and the instruction, and sends back target positions for all arm and finger joints, 30 times a second. How it works Simulation first: build and test your solution in a ready-made Isaac Sim simulation of the robot and our exact scene, with plenty of GPU power from Verda. Real-world data during the event: you don't need any data to get started. During the hackathon, the Aalto University team will publish datasets recorded with the real robot at the venue on Hugging Face, with links shared in the Junction Discord. You can use them for training and for tuning your solution to the real cameras and conditions. Submit a Docker image: your solution runs as a small policy server in a container. Our runner sends its commands to the simulator or the robot through our safety filter. Finals: we evaluate submissions in simulation, on a version of the scene you can't train on, and pick the finalists. They run on the real robot on Sunday. The best real-robot score wins.
